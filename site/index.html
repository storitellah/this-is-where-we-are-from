The project

Photography is more than pressing a button

It is a way to notice, question, remember, and share. In this project, young people photograph the places, people, objects, routines, and moments that shape their lives. Each image should come from curiosity, care, and personal connection.

THIS IS WHERE WE’RE FROM is a participatory youth photography and storytelling project in Kibera, Nairobi, led by photographer Brian Otieno. It builds on his long-term Kibera Stories work, which began in 2013. The project challenges narrow images of Kibera that focus only on poverty, crime, hardship, and neglect. Participants create work that shows everyday life with complexity, dignity, humour, pride, and personal meaning.

For students

You are the photographer here

Nobody is asking you to explain your whole community. You choose what to notice, what to say, and what to keep private. This page tells you what to do each week.

Your week, in five steps
  1. Open this week’s lesson and read the question at the top.
  2. In the session, try the practice with your camera or phone.
  3. Take the assignment home and make the photographs.
  4. Write one or two sentences under each photograph.
  5. Bring them back and talk about what you noticed.
What to bring
  • A camera or a phone
  • A charged battery and space on the memory card
  • A notebook and a pen
  • Water, if the session goes outdoors
  • Your questions

No camera today? Work in a pair. Both names go on the work.

Four rules that never change
  • Ask before you photograph a person.
  • If they say no, that is the whole answer.
  • Never put yourself or anyone else in danger for a photograph.
  • You decide what stays private. So do they.

Before the session
  • Read the lesson and decide which parts your group needs most.
  • Charge batteries, clear memory cards, and check every camera or phone.
  • Print the worksheets for the lesson.
  • Check the route, the timings, and the emergency contacts for outdoor practicals.
  • Confirm consent arrangements for anyone the group plans to photograph.
  • Set out the equipment so the session starts with photography, not admin.
Running the session
  1. Welcome and check-in
  2. Review last week’s assignment
  3. New concept and demonstration
  4. Short break
  5. Guided practice
  6. Group review
  7. Reflection and the new assignment

Keep the review longer than the lecture. Participants learn most from looking at each other’s work.

Adapting the course
  • Run it with any group size. Pair up when equipment is short.
  • Adjust the language and the writing tasks to the ages in the room.
  • Younger groups: shorten the teaching, lengthen the practice.
  • Older groups: push the editing, sequencing, and writing further.
  • Any lesson may be split across two sessions.
  • Allow anyone to pause, skip, or change an assignment without explaining why.
In the group review
  • Describe before interpreting. What is visible comes first.
  • Ask the photographer what they intended before offering an opinion.
  • Use “I notice…” and “my eye goes to…” rather than good or bad.
  • Never make an example of a participant’s weakest image.
  • The photographer always makes the final decision.

How to use it in the session
  1. Agree the running order on the board below. Print the thumbnails if it helps.
  2. Export each chosen photograph at full quality with a clear filename.
  3. Open zineit.app and start a new zine.
  4. Place the cover first, then the spreads in the agreed order.
  5. Add the captions and the quote exactly as they were checked.
  6. Print one proof, fold it, read it, and list the corrections.
  7. Recheck consent for every image and quote before the final export.

Case study

Kibera Stories, by Brian Otieno

One long-term project, studied the way we want our own work studied. Use this in any lesson about storytelling, portraits, editing, or publishing.

Kibera Stories is Brian Otieno’s long-term documentary work about Kibera, made from inside the community he comes from. It began in 2013 and it is still going. Instead of arriving for one dramatic picture and leaving, it follows everyday life over years: work, music, football, faith, food, family, friendship, and change.

That is the difference this course asks you to notice. A photographer who stays becomes part of the story. A photographer who visits takes a picture of the surface.

Look at @KiberaStories
What to look for
  • How close the photographer stands, and what that closeness tells you.
  • Where the light comes from, and what time of day the picture was made.
  • Which pictures show a person, and which show a place.
  • What the captions add that the picture cannot say.
  • What the photographer chose to leave out.
  • How the same subject changes across several years.
Study wall

Empty frames for the reference images you bring to the session — printed pages, screenshots on a laptop, or photographs the group makes in reply. Add them here so the wall belongs to your group.

Talk about it
  • Which picture would you have made differently, and why?
  • What does an outsider miss that you would notice here?
  • Which photograph would you want made about your own street?
  • What would you refuse to photograph, even if you were asked?
Try it yourself
  1. Choose one subject you can return to every week.
  2. Photograph it four times, in four kinds of light.
  3. Write one sentence each time about what changed.
  4. At the end of the month, sequence the four pictures.

This is how a long-term project starts: not with a plan, with a return.

Safety, ethics, and participant care

A photograph is never more important than a person

These principles apply in every lesson, every practical, and every publication decision.

Consent
  • Ask before making close portraits.
  • Explain who you are and what the project is.
  • Explain where the image may appear.
  • Accept no.
  • Allow people to change their mind where possible.
  • Use age-appropriate consent and facilitator guidance for children.
  • Do not assume that being in public removes all ethical responsibility.
Dignity
  • Do not use humiliation, suffering, injury, grief, poverty, or private difficulty as visual decoration.
  • Do not ask people to repeat painful actions for the camera.
  • Do not create false scenes.
  • Do not reduce a person to one difficult moment.
  • Show people as individuals with context and agency.
Privacy
  • Avoid unnecessary exact addresses, school details, phone numbers, documents, medical details, and private family information.
  • Remove sensitive metadata before public sharing where needed.
  • Do not publish a photograph that may expose someone to stigma, punishment, harassment, or danger.
Physical safety
  • Work in groups.
  • Follow agreed routes.
  • Keep emergency contacts.
  • Do not stand in roads, railway areas, open drains, unstable buildings, roofs, restricted areas, or unsafe crowds.
  • Stop walking before looking through the camera.
  • Keep equipment discreet where required.
  • Follow facilitator instructions.
Emotional care
  • Participants may photograph subjects connected to difficult memories.
  • Allow anyone to pause, skip, or change an assignment.
  • Do not force personal disclosure.
  • Use private check-ins when needed.
  • Avoid public critique that embarrasses a participant.
  • Focus feedback on photographs and choices, not personal worth.
Digital safety
  • Do not upload participant images automatically.
  • Keep local drafts private.
  • Explain what export and sharing buttons do.
  • Confirm consent before posting.
  • Do not expose hidden file paths or metadata.
  • Use the Reset Local Data control in the lesson library to clear everything from this device.

About

Led by Brian Otieno, made by the participants

THIS IS WHERE WE’RE FROM works with young people in Kibera, Nairobi. One lesson a week for six months, four lessons a month, with an outdoor practical every month. The young people are not subjects being spoken about. They are photographers and authors telling stories from their own lives. The course runs with any group, of any age, at any size — teachers adapt the language and the assignments to the room in front of them.

This course was developed by Brian Otieno, photographer and founder of Kibera Stories, and it grows out of that long-term work, which began in 2013. It builds photography, writing, editing, sequencing, and presentation skills.

  • A collaborative digital and printed photo-zine
  • A community photography exhibition
